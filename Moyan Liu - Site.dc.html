I use data-driven approaches to inform decision-making in complex systems, with a focus on the Earth's climate. My work combines dynamical systems theory, probabilistic modeling, and AI-based weather models to study the predictability and control of extreme events.

My research journey is the story of a drop of water: from water treatment, to water management, and ultimately to climate and weather. Following this path has broadened my perspective from individual water systems to the interconnected and complex Earth system, shaping how I think about water, climate, and the world around us.

Ongoing project

Weather Jiu-Jitsu

Using the atmosphere’s own instability against a storm: find the moment a dangerous weather system is most sensitive, apply a small perturbation there, and let the physics do the rest.

hero figure — steered vs. unsteered AR track

The idea

Weather is chaotic, which is usually described as bad news: tiny differences grow, and forecasts fall apart. The same sensitivity cuts the other way. If a small difference can grow into a large one, then a carefully chosen small difference could grow into a better one.

The work asks three questions in order. Where is a storm most unstable? What is the smallest nudge that changes its path or intensity there? And how confident can we be that the change is real rather than an artifact of the model?

We work in two settings. Idealized Lorenz systems are small enough to understand completely, so control ideas can be tested honestly. AI weather foundation models are large enough to represent real atmospheric rivers and tropical cyclones, and fast enough to run the thousands of perturbed forecasts this kind of search needs.
